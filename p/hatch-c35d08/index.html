Where the Hatch name comes from

We get addressed as Mr. Hatch frequently. Here is the short version.

In 1948 Fred Hatch and Roy Genthner went into business as H & G Well Drilling. In 1950 Fred started Fredrick J. Hatch Well Drilling.

Fred’s grandson, Joseph Ball Jr., started working for him in 1951. When Fred passed away in December of 1961, Joe and Adney Peck Jr. purchased the company and became partners under the name Hatch Well Drillers.

  • Marc Stevens, Joe’s son-in-law, joined in 1986. He took over Adney’s share of the business in 2005.
  • Elisha Hopkins, Joe’s granddaughter and Marc’s daughter, joined in 2005. She manages the office.
  • Justin Lanphier, Marc’s nephew, became full time in April of 2006. He is our expert in filtration systems and a licensed master driller and pump installer.

Today we use one high-pressure air hammer machine with a hydrofracturing unit, and a service truck with a 2,000 gallon water body and a crane.

Common questions

Answers taken from what the current site already says.

How much does it cost to drill a well?

The price of a well varies greatly depending on the location. It is based on the depth to water and the amount of casing needed. The distance to ledge determines how much casing is needed (state minimum of 20 feet). Give us a call for a free estimate.

How do you pick the location of the well?

The main concern is meeting setbacks from the septic system. The well needs to be 100 feet from the septic field and 60 feet from the tank. Other than that we need a spot that is accessible for our machine.

When should I test the water?

Soon after it is hooked up. The state also recommends testing yearly for bacteria, nitrites and nitrates, and a more comprehensive test every 3 to 5 years. It’s also a good idea to test if you notice a change in the water.

When does hydrofracturing make sense?

When a well is not producing enough water for your needs. We recommend at least 300 feet of hole below the seal. The more hole there is, the better the chance of it working.
